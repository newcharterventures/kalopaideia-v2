Greek
Ancient Hellenic Letters

ἀπόλαυσις

apolausis
noun, fem.
enjoyment, fruition, the act of deriving pleasure or benefit from something
Say: ah-POH-low-sis IPA: /a.ˈpo.lau̯.sis/
Forms

nom. sing. ἀπόλαυσις, gen. ἀπολαύσεως, dat. ἀπολαύσει, acc. ἀπόλαυσιν; plural ἀπολαύσεις

Etymology

From ἀπολαύω (apolauō, 'to enjoy, reap benefit from'), compound of ἀπό ('from') + λαύω ('to enjoy'). The suffix -σις forms an abstract noun denoting the action or result.

In Literature

Aristotle employs ἀπόλαυσις extensively in the Nicomachean Ethics when discussing pleasure and the good life, distinguishing between immediate gratification and genuine enjoyment that accompanies virtuous activity.

In Use

οὐκ ἔστιν ἀπόλαυσις ἀγαθῶν ἄνευ φίλων.

There is no enjoyment of good things without friends.

Did You Know

Epicurus refined this concept to distinguish between kinetic pleasure (active enjoyment, ἀπόλαυσις) and katastematic pleasure (the stable state of contentment), arguing that philosophy's goal was achieving the latter through moderate pursuit of the former.

On Friendship in Aristotle

Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics (Book IX), argues that no one would choose to live without friends, even if they had all other goods. The Greek word ἀπόλαυσις captures something of what is at stake: not mere possession but the active enjoyment that comes only in shared life.

Latin
Classical Roman Letters

sōlācium

noun, neut.
comfort, consolation, solace
Say: so-LAH-kee-oom IPA: /soːˈlaː.ki.um/
Forms

sōlācium, sōlāciī (gen.); plural: sōlācia, sōlāciōrum

Etymology

From sōlārī (to console, comfort), related to sōlor (deponent verb). Cognate with sōlāmen (relief). Root possibly connected to sōlus through the notion of alleviating loneliness or isolation.

In Literature

Cicero uses sōlācium repeatedly in his philosophical works on grief, most notably in the Tusculan Disputations where he discusses consolation for loss. Ovid employs it in the Tristia, writing from exile about the comfort poetry provides in suffering.

In Use

Philosophia mihi sōlācium est in adversīs rēbus.

Philosophy is a comfort to me in adverse circumstances.

Did You Know

The consolatio genre in Roman literature, exemplified by Seneca's letters and Cicero's lost Consolatio on his daughter's death, made sōlācium a central concept in Stoic responses to grief and misfortune.

Cicero on Consolation

After the death of his daughter Tullia in 45 BCE, Cicero composed a now-lost Consolatio to himself. The Latin sōlācium is the word he and Seneca after him would have reached for — comfort that does not deny the loss but accompanies it.

French
The French Letters

flambeau

noun, masc.
torch, flaming brand; figuratively, light of knowledge or inspiration
Say: flahn-BOH IPA: /flɑ̃.bo/
Forms

singular: flambeau; plural: flambeaux

Etymology

From Old French flambel, diminutive of flambe 'flame,' from Latin flammula 'little flame,' itself from flamma 'flame.' The suffix -eau (from Latin -ellus) creates a diminutive.

In Literature

Racine employs flambeau in Phèdre (1677) when the title character speaks of carrying the torch of forbidden passion. The word recurs in Hugo's Les Misérables to describe both literal torches in the Paris sewers and the metaphorical light of progress.

In Use

Le flambeau de la vérité éclaire les ténèbres de l'ignorance.

The torch of truth illuminates the darkness of ignorance.

Did You Know

In 18th-century France, the flambeau was not merely a light source but a symbol of Enlightenment philosophy itself. Voltaire frequently used the image of humanity passing the flambeau from generation to generation to represent the transmission of reason and learning.

The Torch and the Flambeau

In seventeenth-century France, the flambeau was the resinous torch carried in processions and night-time funerals; in eighteenth-century Enlightenment usage, the word turned figurative — flambeau de la raison, the torch of reason.

German
The German Letters

Weltfrömmigkeit

noun, fem.
world-piety; reverent devotion to earthly existence and nature
Say: VELT-fruh-mikh-kite IPA: /ˈvɛltˌfʁœmɪçkaɪ̯t/
Forms

die Weltfrömmigkeit (singular only; gen. der Weltfrömmigkeit, dat. der Weltfrömmigkeit, acc. die Weltfrömmigkeit)

Etymology

Compound of Welt (world, from Old High German weralt) + Frömmigkeit (piety, devotion, from fromm, ultimately from Old High German fruma, meaning advantage, benefit, later righteous). Coined in early Romantic discourse to express religious reverence directed toward nature and earthly life rather than transcendent deity.

In Literature

Goethe employed this concept to describe a spiritual attitude that finds the divine within nature and lived experience rather than in ascetic withdrawal, particularly in his later natural-philosophical writings and conversations with Eckermann.

In Use

Seine Weltfrömmigkeit führte ihn nicht in die Kirche, sondern in die Wälder und Berge.

His world-piety led him not into the church, but into forests and mountains.

Did You Know

Weltfrömmigkeit represents a distinctly German Romantic redefinition of religious feeling, rejecting otherworldly Christianity in favor of immanent spirituality—a concept that deeply influenced later thinkers from Nietzsche to Heidegger in their critiques of metaphysical transcendence.

Goethe's Weltfrömmigkeit

Coined by Goethe in his later years, Weltfrömmigkeit is the reverent devotion to the world itself — to nature, to art, to ordinary human goodness — as the proper object of piety for a thinking modern person.
